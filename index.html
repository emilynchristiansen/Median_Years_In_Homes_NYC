The core of New York City has the shortest-term renters

According to the 2024 ACS PUMS, a U.S. Census Bureau sample survey of demographics, renters in Lower Manhattan, Long Island City, and northern Brooklyn are estimated to have lived in their homes for less time than almost anywhere else in the city.

By Emily Christiansen
10/30/26

The Census Bureau conducts the American Community Survey (ACS) and releases the Public Use Microdata Sample (PUMS) annually to measure demographic characteristics by region. PUMS measures the experiences and characteristics of residents by both weighted household and person samples. This map uses data from the ACS 1-year household survey sample of New York City from 2024. The areas of the map are geographic areas defined by the Census Bureau–Public Use Microdata Areas, or PUMAs–which roughly match community districts and include at least 100,000 people. The variable MV measures when the householder moved into their home, either a house or apartment, and is recorded as a range of time into seven categories: 12 months or less, 13 to 23 months, 2 to 4 years, 5 to 9 years, 10 to 19 years, 20 to 29 years, and 30 years or more. For each PUMA, I estimated the number of renter households in each category by summing household weights. Based on these counts, I calculated the median category (i.e., time range) for each PUMA, for renters only.

The map shows how long the typical renter has lived in their home in each PUMA area. Much of Manhattan, including most of Downtown, all of Midtown, and the Upper West and East Sides, has renters who have lived in their home for a median of two to four years. The Lower East Side and Chinatown are the exception to this trend, where the median years a renter has lived in their home jumps to 10 to 19 years–the median is at least twice that of surrounding areas. Similarly, renters north of Central Park (in Central and West Harlem, Morningside Heights, Hamilton Heights, Washington Heights and Inwood) and the southern Bronx have lived in their homes for a median of 10 to 19 years as well. Southeastern Brooklyn–Canarsie and Flatlands–and the Rockaways in Queens have renters who have lived in their homes for a median of 10 to 19 years as well. North Brooklyn and northwestern Brooklyn have renters who have lived in their homes for the shortest amounts of time. In Queens, renters in Long Island City, Astoria, and a pocket of eastern Queens (Auburndale, Bayside and Douglaston) have lived in their homes for a median of two to four years, the least amount of time in the borough. Staten Island renters have lived in their home for a median of five to nine years.

The core of New York City (with the exception of Auburndale, Bayside and Douglaston in eastern Queens, which have smaller populations1) appears to have renters who have lived in their homes for less time than in outer parts of the city. Notably, these areas also tend to have residents with higher median incomes.2 Along the Harlem River–the southern Bronx and northern Manhattan–and Jamaica Bay, median years lived in a home are highest.

While this is not tied to a current story, it suggests several subjects to investigate further. How strong is the relationship between income and the amount of time one has lived in their home? Does having more wealth allow for more frequent moves, since moving is notoriously expensive? Do renters who live in their homes for more time want to stay in their home, or are they unable to move for financial reasons or otherwise? Is the shorter amount of time lived in a home in the geographic core of New York City a reflection of wealth or a reflection of a greater proportion of transplants? What is the economic impact on an area that has more people moving within it (for example, does it drive up rental prices?)? Further, what is the cultural and physical impact on a space that has more longer-term residents versus shorter-term residents? Can this difference be seen in the physical neighborhoods (like through buildings, types of businesses, infrastructure)? Is there a relationship between type of property (an apartment versus a house) and length of time at that unit? Are there more rent-stabilized or rent-controlled units in areas with higher median years spent in a home? One final question: why do the Lower East Side and Chinatown have renters with a higher median of time lived in their home?

Possible headline with further investigation: Transplant central? The core of New York City has the shortest-term renters
